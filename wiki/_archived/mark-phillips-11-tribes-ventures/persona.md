# Mark Phillips — Investment Memory

This memory covers the Mark Phillips resolved as founder and managing partner of 11 Tribes Ventures, an early-stage venture firm associated with the Chicago area; it excludes same-name people. The strongest material is Phillips’s first-person writing and firm updates. Podcast descriptions, profiles, and aggregators are treated as source-reported context only. Several pages lack publication dates, multiple pages repeat the same interview, and numerous audio files lacked transcripts. Current-sounding claims are therefore limited to their source dates. The record principally describes 11 Tribes’ institutional strategy, not Phillips’s personal investing.

## founder_team

Team quality dominates the initial idea. Phillips expressly prefers an “A-team with a B-idea” to the reverse, and his shorthand is that success is more a function of “who” than “how.” His diligence treats the founder as the central asset: 11 Tribes reports using Culture Index psychometric profiling during investment diligence and portfolio-leadership evaluation. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0010] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0026] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0047]

The explicit positive screen is distinctive founder–market fit. He also prefers cultures that are simultaneously highly challenging and highly supportive. Inferred from his leadership writing—not an independently stated investment gate—he likely values self-awareness, humility, active listening, empowerment, action under uncertainty, and responsibility for what works rather than protecting the appearance of being right. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0027] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0029] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0058] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0059] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0061] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0062] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0071]

Founder wellbeing is part of the underwriting logic, not an ancillary benefit. Phillips says founder outcomes are foundational to every investment and that financial returns should not come at the expense of founder wellbeing. 11 Tribes allocates 2% of every invested dollar to coaching, counseling, organizational health, and related resilience support; Phillips calls founder development the firm’s highest-ROI investment. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0005] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0006] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0040] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0063] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0067]

Care is paired with accountability. The firm says it names problems early, establishes commitments and timelines, assumes excellence, and holds founders accountable to their chosen outcomes. Its model depends on sufficient trust to discuss leadership gaps, blind spots, and necessary change. Inferred diligence questions are whether a founder can separate company performance from personal identity, receive direct feedback without evasion, and honor explicit commitments. These are tentative applications of portfolio practices, not quoted prerequisites. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0016] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0017] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0018] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0019] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0020]

This emphasis has a reported biographical origin but is not independent validation of the strategy: sources say Phillips’s diabetes-device startup failed following financing difficulties and competitive displacement, prompting an identity crisis that helped shape the “capital and care” model. [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0057] [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0058] [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0059]

## market_opportunity

Phillips explicitly seeks niche markets with relatively few competitors. A market ceiling is not automatically disqualifying: he cited National Quality Systems’ roughly 2,400-hospital trauma-registry market as attractive precisely because other investors considered it too small. The relevant question is whether the attainable market supports an appropriately sized fund outcome, not whether it supports a billion-dollar company. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0027] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0048]

His return model favors a broader buyer universe at moderate exit sizes. He argues there are roughly ten times as many buyers for a $100 million company as for a $1 billion one and that businesses around $50 million can sometimes produce cash-on-cash returns comparable with hypergrowth startups. He is not opposed to billion-dollar outcomes; he opposes requiring them where a smaller outcome can produce exceptional returns. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0032] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0033] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0041] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0050]

His most specific forward view is that the next decade may produce fewer unicorns and more $100–250 million acquisitions, especially in vertical AI software where domain depth can beat scale. An outside profile similarly reports a focus on niche, non-coastal markets and potential $80–250 million exits, while a podcast description gives $75–250 million. Those contextual ranges are orientation, not universal gates. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0049] [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0107] [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0074]

## competition_defensibility

He prefers less crowded niches and is skeptical of consensus trades. In AI, his objection is not the technology but undisciplined competition for the “next big” company and the inflated pricing that follows. This produces a selective, not categorical, AI posture: out on frenzy-priced, undifferentiated opportunities; potentially in on capital-efficient vertical applications with domain depth and appropriately sized outcomes. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0021] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0027] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0077] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0079]

His newer AI-era moat thesis is that possessing data or knowledge is insufficient because those inputs are becoming broadly available. Defensibility instead comes from systems that generate, process, and learn from data better than competitors. An inferred diligence application is to test the feedback system—how proprietary learning is produced and operationalized—rather than accept a static data claim as the moat. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0012]

Domain expertise matters in practice. In the NQS case, source notes say operating partner Carolyn Wilson, formerly a health-system COO, supplied healthcare expertise during diligence. This supports an investor-fit preference for opportunities where 11 Tribes’ operators or LP network can improve judgment and company development, although one case does not establish a universal sector prerequisite. [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0005] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0007]

## product_solution

Phillips’s broad prescription is to invest in companies that genuinely create value, but the record supplies no universal product-development framework or mandatory maturity threshold. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0035]

The strongest product-market illustration is NQS. Source notes report 16 signed hospital contracts at financing close even though launch remained six to eight weeks away; the source characterized this as unusual validation of urgency and founder credibility. This is strong validation in a particular investment, not evidence that pre-launch contracts are always required. [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0004]

Inferred from his operating advice, he may favor founders who act and experiment through fear instead of waiting for confidence, but this should not become a universal product-speed test. His repeated point is that action reduces fear and creates forward motion. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0013] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0014]

## traction_growth

The central growth preference is explicit: sustainable, durable, profitable growth rather than growth at any cost. Phillips presents this as both a needed venture strategy and the model for “enduring companies.” By 2025 he said the thesis was maturing around capital-efficient founders and business models, with more reasonable growth expectations contributing to higher portfolio success. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0003] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0030] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0031] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0055] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0056]

This does not mean every company must already be profitable. Phillips calls for clarity about the timing and prioritization of profitability, while outside descriptions say he helps entrepreneurs scale responsibly and intentionally. The diligence issue is whether management has a credible path and sequencing logic, not whether one profitability milestone has already been crossed. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0034] [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0039]

Reported fund performance is manager-supplied, not independently verified. In March 2024 Phillips reported Fund I at 2.7x MOIC and 62.7% IRR across 33 companies; in April 2025 he said it had entered harvest and was tracking in the top quartile for its vintage. These figures contextualize his confidence but should not be treated as audited results. [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0078] [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0120]

## business_model_economics

Capital efficiency is among the clearest selection criteria. Phillips explicitly seeks “intense” capital efficiency, targets founders and business models with capital-efficient mindsets, favors founders for whom one or two further rounds are optional rather than assumed, and advocates thoughtful, properly sized capital infusions. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0027] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0045] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0055] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0081]

Ownership alignment matters. He backs founders who want to grow profitably and retain ownership, while a podcast description reports that he warns entrepreneurs about surrendering ownership too early. Financing dependence is therefore a risk to examine, not an automatic refusal rule. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0044] [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0037]

The economics are calibrated to moderate exits. AI may enable smaller businesses that need less capital and choose an approximately $100 million exit on a reasonable timeline. This suggests an underwriting loop of constrained entry price, modest capital need, founder ownership, credible profitability, and an exit with many plausible buyers. That combined loop is a synthesis; its constituent preferences are directly supported. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0023] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0043] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0044] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0050]

## deal_terms_valuation

Price discipline is explicit and persistent. Phillips says 11 Tribes does not chase the hottest deals, underwrites to value, and frequently pays below the prevailing revenue multiple. He links the 2020–2022 hype to inflated rounds and says the firm avoided and continues to avoid them. His sharpest example is a pre-revenue AI company at a $50 million valuation, where he believes prospective value has already been priced out. This is a specific negative example, not a universal valuation ceiling. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0009] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0022] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0043]

He describes himself as a Midwest investor with a different definition of price, implying greater price sensitivity without supplying a general multiple or ownership threshold. Third-party profiles disagree on check size: Signal reports $100,000–$300,000 with a $200,000 sweet spot, while The Pitch reports $5,000–$250,000. The latter includes show-related investments, so neither range establishes the definitive institutional mandate. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0028] [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0067] [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0085] [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0086]

Phillips wrote in June 2026 that 11 Tribes leads virtually every round and uses a lead position and board seat to influence growth philosophy. This newer first-person statement conflicts with VC Sheet’s undated report that the firm does not lead. The direct statement is stronger evidence of stated practice as of June 2026, but deal-specific confirmation remains necessary. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0046] [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0109] [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0110]

## timing

Phillips rejects claims of precise market foresight: no one truly knows where markets are headed, so the response is discipline, patience, and opportunism. Yet he has made time-specific allocation calls, calling the then-current period a terrific time for venture and later describing the vintage as potentially the best of the next decade. These statements coexist if read as selective deployment amid uncertainty, not certainty about market timing. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0008] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0037] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0069]

His AI timing view likewise has two sides: consensus enthusiasm can make fashionable rounds unattractive, while the same technology can create capital-efficient vertical businesses suited to moderate acquisitions. The opportunity lies in business formation and operating leverage, not indiscriminate exposure to the “AI” label. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0076] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0078] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0079]

## investor_fit_constraints

The preferred partnership is high-touch. Phillips positions small, agile funds as operationally minded partners rather than financiers alone, deliberately keeps 11 Tribes small enough to meet founder needs, and regards its LP base as a portfolio asset. Fit is strongest when a founder wants candid engagement, resilience resources, network access, and accountability rather than only a passive check. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0004] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0007] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0015] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0024]

The intended portfolio construction is concentrated at 20–25 companies, although a March 2024 update reported 33 Fund I portfolio companies; the sources may describe different funds, vehicles, or an evolved architecture and do not resolve the discrepancy. The firm also expected most investments to be QSBS-qualifying stock held for five years, indicating a long-hold orientation without proving that every deal must qualify. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0042] [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0078] [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0080]

Industry boundaries exist because Phillips refers to entrepreneurs in industries “we don’t invest in,” but the first-person evidence does not enumerate a complete mandate. An outside profile lists SaaS, fintech, health, consumer, and enterprise, while another biography says early-stage technology in industries ripe for disruption. These descriptions should guide routing, not replace current mandate confirmation. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0036] [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0108] [ctx:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0013]

Inferred tentative decision policy: strong “in” patterns are an exceptional team with distinctive founder–market fit; a challenging and supportive culture; a niche or bounded market with limited competition; genuine value creation; capital-efficient growth with a credible profitability path; limited dependence on repeated financing; sensible entry valuation; and an achievable exit that works without requiring a unicorn. Partnership fit improves when the founder welcomes direct feedback and active, board-level support. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0010] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0027] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0029] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0044] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0045] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0046]

Strong “out” patterns are growth at all costs, hype-priced or consensus deals, a pre-revenue AI valuation around the cited $50 million example, financing plans that assume serial rounds, and—Inferred from the support model—founders who seek only capital while resisting candid engagement. The final point is not an expressly stated rejection rule. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0039] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0043] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0045] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0073] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0078]

Questions explicitly or closely supported by the record include: Why is this founder uniquely suited to the market? How much capital is actually required? Is another round an option or an assumption? When and how should profitability become the priority? What genuine value does the company create? What buyer universe exists at a moderate outcome? For AI, what system continuously generates, processes, and learns from data better than competitors? For leadership, can difficult issues be named early and converted into commitments and timelines? ## Distinctive / doesn't-fit-the-taxonomy

The distinctive feature is the attempt to make founder formation an investable risk-control system. Phillips treats mental, emotional, physical, and spiritual development as contributing to a more sustainable and profitable portfolio, while 11 Tribes reserves capital for coaching, counseling, and organizational health. This is presented as part of financial underwriting and people-risk mitigation, not merely conventional founder services. [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0025] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0040] [ev:mark-phillips-11-tribes-ventures-0063]

His broader human philosophy is relevant but belongs outside hard screening taxonomy. He emphasizes persistence through suffering, relationships despite vulnerability, action amid fear, values alignment, purpose, and a personal identity larger than company performance. These writings explain the moral vocabulary behind “capital and care,” but most are inferred signals rather than explicit investment requirements.
