# Michael Hyatt — Investment Memory

This memory concerns the Canadian technology entrepreneur and investor who co-founded BlueCat and Dyadem, works through Hyatt Family Office, and became a Northleaf Capital Partners Senior Advisor—not Michael S. Hyatt, the publishing executive. Identity is supported by the linked investor profile and Northleaf’s company-specific biography. [ctx:michael-hyatt-0120] [ctx:michael-hyatt-0122]

The record mixes direct remarks, interviews, company announcements, first-party social posts, press reporting, and lower-confidence aggregators. Much of the interview material lacks publication dates; current-sounding positions should therefore be read as applying when the source was recorded, not necessarily today. Repeated pages often reproduce the same interview and are not independent corroboration. Operating lessons become investment rules below only when Hyatt explicitly makes that connection; otherwise they are marked **Inferred**.

## founder_team

Hyatt’s strongest explicit direct-investment filter is the person. He says founder or CEO quality is the only near-universal correlation he sees across his historical investments, and elsewhere describes direct startup investing as choosing the “jockey,” then helping the company find its way. [ev:michael-hyatt-0310] [ev:michael-hyatt-0161] [ev:michael-hyatt-0162]

**In:** founders he genuinely likes, believes in, and can imagine working beside for roughly a decade. He describes early-stage investing as a marriage or shared foxhole and sets unusually high trust and character standards: humility, openness, kindness, ethical conduct, and respect for employees and customers. [ev:michael-hyatt-0017] [ev:michael-hyatt-0026] [ev:michael-hyatt-0033] [ev:michael-hyatt-0034] [ev:michael-hyatt-0313] [ev:michael-hyatt-0314] [ev:michael-hyatt-0315]

**Out:** people he dislikes or does not believe in, however intelligent; a discovered fraud charge is an expressly stated disqualifier. [ev:michael-hyatt-0018] [ev:michael-hyatt-0113]

Adaptability is central because he expects the company at exit to differ from the starting concept. His explicit questions are: Can the founder work constructively with him? Is the founder ambitious? Can the founder pivot? Will the founder listen, absorb valid feedback, debate honestly, and acknowledge being wrong? [ev:michael-hyatt-0040] [ev:michael-hyatt-0041] [ev:michael-hyatt-0042] [ev:michael-hyatt-0283] [ev:michael-hyatt-0284] [ev:michael-hyatt-0131] [ev:michael-hyatt-0133] [ev:michael-hyatt-0134]

Ambition must coexist with flexibility. He wants partners seeking to build a large, consequential company, hire people, and make a difference; founders without that aspiration are not a fit. [ev:michael-hyatt-0020] [ev:michael-hyatt-0282]

He also wants operators who can run the company without being taught basic entrepreneurship. [ev:michael-hyatt-0156] [ev:michael-hyatt-0191] **Inferred:** discipline, perseverance, uncommon tolerance for risk and adversity, fact-based realism, strong hiring, and delegation are favorable indicators because he repeatedly presents them as essential operating qualities, not because every source establishes them as formal gates. [ev:michael-hyatt-0012] [ev:michael-hyatt-0013] [ev:michael-hyatt-0015] [ev:michael-hyatt-0143] [ev:michael-hyatt-0089] [ev:michael-hyatt-0090]

His own founder history informs this lens: he handled sales while Richard Hyatt supplied product, design, coding, and technical strength. [ev:michael-hyatt-0044] [ctx:michael-hyatt-0019] **Inferred:** he is likely attentive to complementary founder skill sets and whether leaders hire around their weaknesses, but this should not be turned into a required team configuration. [ev:michael-hyatt-0144] [ev:michael-hyatt-0145]

## market_opportunity

**In:** large and growing markets with real product-market fit. Hyatt explicitly says he would prefer a relatively weaker team in a big market with product-market fit to a better team in a poor opportunity, and later summarizes his preferred combination as a large market, people he likes, and reasonable terms. [ev:michael-hyatt-0085] [ev:michael-hyatt-0068]

The opportunity must solve a material problem for which customers allocate scarce budget. In enterprise markets, he views the competitive set as every item on the buyer’s quarterly priority list, not merely products in the same category. [ev:michael-hyatt-0083] [ev:michael-hyatt-0087] [ev:michael-hyatt-0125] [ev:michael-hyatt-0126]

**Inferred:** founder-originated problems and currently tolerated frictions can be fertile opportunity sources. Hyatt advises entrepreneurs to start with a genuine problem, especially one personally experienced, rather than inventing a company and searching afterward for a use. [ev:michael-hyatt-0152] [ev:michael-hyatt-0147] [ev:michael-hyatt-0149] [ev:michael-hyatt-0054] This is operating advice, not a universal rejection rule for founders who did not personally experience the problem.

He explicitly prefers B2B and has highlighted enterprise sales, telemedicine, digital transformation, logistics, cybersecurity, cloud, AI, and quantum-enabling technologies. [ev:michael-hyatt-0010] [ev:michael-hyatt-0154] [ev:michael-hyatt-0327] [ev:michael-hyatt-0139] [ev:michael-hyatt-0174] B2C is not absolutely excluded—he acknowledges others can build it—but he sees it as demanding substantial capital and rapid scale. [ev:michael-hyatt-0011]

Geographically, his operating advice favors selling into the United States because of customer size, speed, and willingness to try new products, while he has also repeatedly described Canada as an attractive place to build technology companies. [ev:michael-hyatt-0064] [ev:michael-hyatt-0118] [ev:michael-hyatt-0192]

## competition_defensibility

Hyatt explicitly seeks businesses that are both scalable and defensible. [ev:michael-hyatt-0153] [ev:michael-hyatt-0187] Strong intellectual property contributed to his positive view of DataStealth, while unresolved IP ownership can stop an investment after an attractive pitch. [ev:michael-hyatt-0071] [ev:michael-hyatt-0038] [ev:michael-hyatt-0109]

For AI, he wants a non-obvious insight and order-of-magnitude improvement: something genuinely “zero to one,” roughly ten times better or materially cheaper, rather than a 20–30% enhancement. [ev:michael-hyatt-0061] [ev:michael-hyatt-0062] [ev:michael-hyatt-0226] [ev:michael-hyatt-0228] Analogy-led positioning such as “the Uber of” or “the ChatGPT of” is a warning sign, and merely using AI is not a complete value proposition. [ev:michael-hyatt-0231] [ev:michael-hyatt-0232]

**Inferred:** sticky niche positioning, high gross margins, proprietary data, and data-driven decision-making may strengthen defensibility in his assessment, but these comments are operating observations rather than universal investment prerequisites. [ev:michael-hyatt-0206] [ev:michael-hyatt-0150] [ev:michael-hyatt-0151]

## product_solution

Hyatt includes hands-on product diligence: he tries the product or service and asks people in his network to use it and describe the experience. [ev:michael-hyatt-0036] [ev:michael-hyatt-0037]

The strongest product proof is not an investor’s opinion but a customer’s willingness to buy and then buy more. An MVP and talented builders without customers may be interesting, but he is unsure that they constitute a company. [ev:michael-hyatt-0008] [ev:michael-hyatt-0009]

His explicit product questions include: Does the customer urgently need it? Does it receive budget? Does it embody a secret others have missed? Does it deliver a tenfold improvement? Can users deploy it simply, and does it reliably work? [ev:michael-hyatt-0083] [ev:michael-hyatt-0126] [ev:michael-hyatt-0226] [ev:michael-hyatt-0166] [ev:michael-hyatt-0167]

**Inferred:** he favors focused products and repeatable use cases; he advises doing one thing well and warns that pursuing five initiatives generally weakens execution. [ev:michael-hyatt-0030] [ev:michael-hyatt-0031] [ev:michael-hyatt-0084] This is not evidence that a multiproduct company is automatically out.

## traction_growth

Revenue, paying customers, retention, and account expansion are Hyatt’s primary de-risking signals. He repeatedly identifies “land and expand”—a customer buying and purchasing more in a later period—as the best evidence that a real company exists. [ev:michael-hyatt-0005] [ev:michael-hyatt-0006] [ev:michael-hyatt-0217] [ev:michael-hyatt-0218] [ev:michael-hyatt-0262]

He prefers market pull to demand manufactured solely through sales effort. [ev:michael-hyatt-0086] Enterprise growth nevertheless requires rigorous sales execution and the ability to scale large-account selling. [ev:michael-hyatt-0088]

Growth quality matters more than headline pace. Revenue expansion with stable gross margin suggests the company is not simply buying market share; accelerating growth without excessive cash burn can justify doubling down with additional capital. [ev:michael-hyatt-0324] [ev:michael-hyatt-0325] Conversely, very high growth paired with equally high losses prompts him to ask whether the business is purchasing revenue, market share, or gross margin. [ev:michael-hyatt-0076]

During the 2020 shock, he viewed one or two post-shock quarters of scale and growth as meaningful evidence because pre-shock revenue had become less informative. [ev:michael-hyatt-0309] This was a dated crisis-specific observation, not a permanent numerical diligence gate.

## business_model_economics

**In:** enterprise sales, recurring revenue, strong gross margins, sound unit economics, and a credible path to profitability. [ev:michael-hyatt-0154] [ev:michael-hyatt-0075] [ev:michael-hyatt-0251] He asks whether the company can ever produce a net margin and argues that weak or negative gross margins can make a business mathematically nonviable. [ev:michael-hyatt-0207] [ev:michael-hyatt-0208] [ev:michael-hyatt-0257]

He rejects free-product, land-grab, or eyeball-first pitches that lack credible economics. [ev:michael-hyatt-0210] [ev:michael-hyatt-0254] He prefers 50% growth at break-even to 100% growth dependent on continuing losses and, in a tight financing environment, a smaller profitable company to a larger loss-making one. [ev:michael-hyatt-0077] [ev:michael-hyatt-0223]

Capital is attractive when it has a productive use—such as accelerating revenue while improving gross margin—and destructive when it funds low-quality growth or is raised first and assigned a use later. [ev:michael-hyatt-0213] [ev:michael-hyatt-0214] [ev:michael-hyatt-0258] His preference for slower profitable growth is partly a financing-risk warning: markets cycle, and companies unable to reach positive cash flow may face punitive funding rather than an automatic refusal from Hyatt. [ev:michael-hyatt-0078] [ev:michael-hyatt-0221]

A historical threshold appears in a 2020 interview: he said great technology companies need gross margins above 70%, with the best public examples above 80%. [ev:michael-hyatt-0326] This is a dated sector benchmark, not a universal mandate for every business model.

## deal_terms_valuation

Price can spoil an otherwise good company. Hyatt says VCs sometimes select the right founders and businesses at the wrong valuation and receive a return smaller than expected. [ev:michael-hyatt-0211] [ev:michael-hyatt-0255]

**Out:** very early companies with little beyond a deck and valuations that make the return arithmetic implausible. [ev:michael-hyatt-0066] [ev:michael-hyatt-0067] He has cited $20–50 million entry valuations as risky against what he characterized as many $50–100 million exits, because the required several-hundred-million-dollar outcome becomes exceptionally unlikely. [ev:michael-hyatt-0073] These figures describe his distributional argument, not fixed universal caps.

He favors reasonable terms that allow a meaningful check and has historically avoided investing when broad markets appeared too expensive, even if that meant holding cash. [ev:michael-hyatt-0068] [ev:michael-hyatt-0306]

Hyatt warns against punitive structured financings, including large liquidation preferences and cap discounts, because founders’ common equity can be eviscerated below a very large exit. [ev:michael-hyatt-0220] [ev:michael-hyatt-0265] Yet he also values VC-led diligence and terms that align investors, and likes investing beside a VC that can provide board oversight and protect rights over time. [ev:michael-hyatt-0321] [ev:michael-hyatt-0322]

He personally prefers private-equity structures with larger stakes and control, disliking fragmented minority ownership with no ability to correct management or strategy if performance deteriorates. [ev:michael-hyatt-0096] [ev:michael-hyatt-0097]

## timing

Hyatt repeatedly argues that bad macro periods can be good company-formation periods because competitors retreat, attention falls, valuations reset, and known bad news is already reflected. [ev:michael-hyatt-0001] [ev:michael-hyatt-0053] [ev:michael-hyatt-0307]

His technology outlook has been consistently expansive but sometimes prematurely precise. He saw the pandemic compressing years of digital transformation into roughly one year, viewed internet, cloud, and AI as early, and later described AI as transformative but immature. [ev:michael-hyatt-0016] [ev:michael-hyatt-0043] [ev:michael-hyatt-0056] [ev:michael-hyatt-0229] In the June 2023 discussion, he expected another one or two years of AI germination; later comments call AI underrated while simultaneously describing an AI bubble and widespread valuation silliness. [ctx:michael-hyatt-0189] [ev:michael-hyatt-0230] [ev:michael-hyatt-0105] [ev:michael-hyatt-0059] [ev:michael-hyatt-0060]

The resulting policy is selective rather than sector-blind: believe in AI’s long-run disproportionate winners, especially in areas such as healthcare and search, while rejecting gimmicks, weak utility, and unjustified prices. [ev:michael-hyatt-0058] [ev:michael-hyatt-0063] [ev:michael-hyatt-0225]

Some older forecasts—autonomous driving becoming ubiquitous within two years and household batteries spreading within seven—show a tendency toward aggressive timing predictions and should not be treated as reliable present forecasts. [ev:michael-hyatt-0046] [ev:michael-hyatt-0047]

## investor_fit_constraints

Hyatt is a concentrated, hands-on investor. He writes larger checks into fewer deals, prefers opportunities where his operating experience can disproportionately help, and may sit on boards, advise, and lean into execution. [ev:michael-hyatt-0069] [ev:michael-hyatt-0070] [ev:michael-hyatt-0022]

His value-add is complementary with Richard Hyatt: Michael emphasizes business, sales, and go-to-market while Richard contributes technical depth. [ev:michael-hyatt-0052] He may intentionally bring helpful friends into a round even when he could finance it alone. [ev:michael-hyatt-0023]

Time and energy, not merely capital, constrain his direct portfolio. A good person and good idea may still be out if he will not commit sustained attention; he says he is not actively searching for deal flow and can wait years without investing. [ev:michael-hyatt-0019] [ev:michael-hyatt-0021] [ev:michael-hyatt-0024] [ev:michael-hyatt-0025] In one description, about 100 inbound decks are reduced to roughly seven conversations using founder, market, and competitive research; these are workflow figures, not acceptance-rate promises. [ev:michael-hyatt-0111] [ev:michael-hyatt-0112] [ev:michael-hyatt-0115]

He distinguishes family-office wealth preservation from entrepreneurial wealth creation. The family office says no frequently, does not seek multiple venture losses to produce one outlier, and accepts missing great deals as the price of discipline. [ev:michael-hyatt-0158] [ev:michael-hyatt-0074] [ev:michael-hyatt-0203] He also recognizes that entrepreneurial drive, spontaneity, and action bias can be liabilities in slow, consistent investing. [ev:michael-hyatt-0091] [ev:michael-hyatt-0092] [ev:michael-hyatt-0093]

For fund selection, source reporting says he favors managers with substantial personal capital at risk; he also scores quarterly fund statements and compares results with market quartiles. [ctx:michael-hyatt-0094] [ev:michael-hyatt-0114]

## Distinctive / doesn't-fit-the-taxonomy

Hyatt’s framework contains a deliberate tension: he says the entrepreneur matters more than the original idea because early companies pivot, yet he also elevates large-market product-market fit above team competence. [ev:michael-hyatt-0161] [ev:michael-hyatt-0040] [ev:michael-hyatt-0085] The practical synthesis is not “team or market”; it is a founder he trusts to navigate change inside an opportunity where customers already reveal consequential demand. [ev:michael-hyatt-0134] [ev:michael-hyatt-0009]

His private-company activity also spans minority angel investing, VC co-investment, fund exposure, and majority-control acquisitions; no single stage or governance template captures it. [ctx:michael-hyatt-0017] [ctx:michael-hyatt-0029] [ev:michael-hyatt-0096]

He is unusually candid that successful entrepreneurship does not confer investing competence. His memory should therefore preserve both the operator’s appetite for ambitious, disruptive companies and the family office’s boring, selective wealth-preservation discipline. [ev:michael-hyatt-0157] [ev:michael-hyatt-0158] [ev:michael-hyatt-0159]

A tentative **Inferred diligence sequence** is: first test integrity, trust, compatibility, ambition, humility, and pivot capacity; then determine whether a large market is pulling a focused solution through actual budget, purchase, retention, and expansion; inspect IP ownership and direct product experience; examine gross margin, unit economics, cash requirements, debt, and financing structure; finally test whether the entry price leaves a realistic return and whether Hyatt’s operating contribution is genuinely differentiated.
