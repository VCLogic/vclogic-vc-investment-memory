# Michael Hyatt — Investment Memory

This memory concerns the Canadian technology entrepreneur and investor who co-founded Dyadem and BlueCat, later established the Hyatt Family Office, acquired control of DataStealth with Richard Hyatt, and became a Senior Advisor to Northleaf Capital Partners. It excludes the same-name publishing executive. The source base mixes direct speech, first-person posts, company announcements, interviews, profiles, and weaker aggregators. Preferences differ by capital bucket and have evolved with market conditions; operating advice is marked Inferred when translated into a possible selection lens. Dates are included only when supplied explicitly, and all current-sounding roles should be read as of their respective source dates.

## founder_team

Hyatt’s strongest recurring direct-investment preference is for the entrepreneur rather than the initial idea. He expects strategies to change, so he looks for people he likes, trusts, and could work beside for roughly a decade. Personal character is a gate: brilliance does not offset objectionable conduct, and a poor working relationship is enough for him to decline. He screens for trustworthiness, humility, openness, kindness, respectful treatment of stakeholders, and the ability to disagree constructively. [ev:michael-hyatt-0016] [ev:michael-hyatt-0017] [ev:michael-hyatt-0185] [ev:michael-hyatt-0380] [ev:michael-hyatt-0381] [ev:michael-hyatt-0385]

Adaptability and coachability are central. He wants founders who can recognize valid feedback, change direction when circumstances demand it, and make difficult decisions. In an initial meeting he may deliberately challenge the founder to observe the response, while acknowledging that he may be wrong. Dismissing well-grounded feedback signals weak judgment; intelligent certainty is not a substitute for self-awareness. [ev:michael-hyatt-0038] [ev:michael-hyatt-0039] [ev:michael-hyatt-0127] [ev:michael-hyatt-0128] [ev:michael-hyatt-0262] [ev:michael-hyatt-0264] [ev:michael-hyatt-0265]

He also wants ambition proportionate to the relationship: founders seeking to build consequential companies and large employers, with a realistic willingness to sustain the effort for many years. He favors ambition combined with flexibility and wants founders to know what role and destination they ultimately want. Once aligned, he says he supports the founder’s chosen exit timing rather than forcing his own preferred path, although he separately stresses that investors ultimately have obligations to return capital. [ev:michael-hyatt-0018] [ev:michael-hyatt-0131] [ev:michael-hyatt-0132] [ev:michael-hyatt-0133] [ev:michael-hyatt-0314] [ev:michael-hyatt-0316] [ev:michael-hyatt-0159] [ev:michael-hyatt-0160]

Inferred from repeated operating advice, he likely responds positively to perseverance, resilience, sustained effort, strong hiring, delegation, and a low-ego culture. He cautions that persistence cannot eliminate luck, that companies generally take longer and cost more than expected, and that founders must hire specialists who cover their weaknesses and then give them real authority. These are useful diligence lenses, not all stated prerequisites. [ev:michael-hyatt-0011] [ev:michael-hyatt-0013] [ev:michael-hyatt-0083] [ev:michael-hyatt-0143] [ev:michael-hyatt-0144] [ev:michael-hyatt-0263] [ev:michael-hyatt-0382]

His founder-first stance does not mean the market is secondary in all circumstances. He says an unattractive market can defeat an excellent team, and his explicit criteria combine founder quality, market quality, receptiveness to feedback, and adaptability. [ev:michael-hyatt-0129] [ev:michael-hyatt-0261]

## market_opportunity

He favors substantial, growing, underserved markets where his active involvement can create meaningful upside. A strong team in a weak market is insufficient, while large markets, reasonable terms, compatible people, and meaningful ownership or check size reinforce one another. [ev:michael-hyatt-0068] [ev:michael-hyatt-0261] [ev:michael-hyatt-0334]

His clearest domain preference is B2B. He is attracted to enterprise businesses with strong margins, recurring revenue, scalability, defensibility, and a significant customer problem. He is wary of consumer businesses that require enormous capital before attractive economics appear, although his documented The Pitch investment in a consumer-technology marketplace shows that this is a preference rather than an absolute exclusion. [ev:michael-hyatt-0009] [ev:michael-hyatt-0010] [ev:michael-hyatt-0177] [ev:michael-hyatt-0178] [ctx:michael-hyatt-0174] [ctx:michael-hyatt-0175]

Security, cloud, and AI recur as favorable long-term categories. He has described security as attractive, said internet, cloud, and AI remain early, and expressed bullishness about cloud, security, and AI companies. In other supplied material, he emphasized that crowded AI markets will produce many losers and that technology change makes selection unusually difficult. [ev:michael-hyatt-0041] [ev:michael-hyatt-0042] [ev:michael-hyatt-0138] [ev:michael-hyatt-0272] [ev:michael-hyatt-0282]

He is optimistic about AI’s broad impact but discriminates between foundational value and fashionable packaging. He expects a small number of substantive AI companies—potentially in healthcare and search—to capture disproportionate outcomes, while regarding many entrants as gimmicky or overvalued. [ev:michael-hyatt-0057] [ev:michael-hyatt-0059] [ev:michael-hyatt-0060] [ev:michael-hyatt-0064]

His domain boundary is competence rather than fashion: he largely stays within areas he understands and expressly avoids clean technology and health technology because he lacks sufficient knowledge. This creates tension with his thematic optimism about AI-enabled healthcare and earlier favorable comments about telemedicine; the evidence supports interest in a trend, not necessarily willingness to underwrite a company personally. [ev:michael-hyatt-0279] [ev:michael-hyatt-0280] [ev:michael-hyatt-0064] [ev:michael-hyatt-0396]

Inferred from operating commentary, he is attracted to painful, urgent problems and demand-led markets. His model of strong product-market fit is a necessity tied to preventing serious harm, not superficial enthusiasm, and he favors customer pull over seller push. [ev:michael-hyatt-0043] [ev:michael-hyatt-0084] [ev:michael-hyatt-0085] [ev:michael-hyatt-0088]

## competition_defensibility

Hyatt explicitly prefers scalable businesses with defensible positions. Intellectual property matters: strong IP supported his favorable view of DataStealth, while discovered IP defects can make an otherwise attractive opportunity uninvestable. Unclear IP ownership is also a material diligence problem. [ev:michael-hyatt-0036] [ev:michael-hyatt-0072] [ev:michael-hyatt-0177] [ev:michael-hyatt-0204] [ev:michael-hyatt-0110]

His favored differentiation is not marginal. He has used an approximately tenfold improvement in cost or product performance as the marker of an exceptional opportunity and argues that modest incremental improvement is unlikely to create a winner. For AI specifically, he wants a paradigm shift and roughly order-of-magnitude cost improvement rather than a thin ChatGPT wrapper. [ev:michael-hyatt-0061] [ev:michael-hyatt-0062] [ev:michael-hyatt-0063] [ev:michael-hyatt-0240] [ev:michael-hyatt-0242] [ev:michael-hyatt-0244]

Competition includes the customer’s entire spending-priority stack, not just direct rivals. His screening examines category crowding, prior comparable-company success, and possible saturation. Inferred diligence questions are therefore: Is this among the customer’s highest-priority purchases? Is the category already crowded? Have analogous companies demonstrated viable outcomes? [ev:michael-hyatt-0089] [ev:michael-hyatt-0267] [ev:michael-hyatt-0338] [ev:michael-hyatt-0386]

A sticky niche with high gross margins can be attractive, as can effective use of proprietary data. Those observations support a defensibility lens but do not establish that data ownership or a niche is universally required. [ev:michael-hyatt-0174] [ev:michael-hyatt-0175] [ev:michael-hyatt-0221]

## product_solution

Customer value outranks technology labels. For AI businesses, Hyatt wants concrete value and customer delight; AI is an enabling component, not a sufficient company thesis. In 2023 he regarded many AI offerings as demonstrations without enough practical utility and expected the market to need another one or two years to mature. [ev:michael-hyatt-0241] [ev:michael-hyatt-0243] [ev:michael-hyatt-0246] [ev:michael-hyatt-0281]

He favors products that generate an immediate, strong positive reaction and can be tested in actual use. His early-stage diligence may place a live product with people in his network and collect their reactions. DataStealth’s functional technology, straightforward deployment, and low-friction installation exemplify the qualities he praises. [ev:michael-hyatt-0035] [ev:michael-hyatt-0044] [ev:michael-hyatt-0189] [ev:michael-hyatt-0248] [ev:michael-hyatt-0249]

Inferred from operating advice, product focus matters: do one thing well, avoid divided attention, begin with a compelling problem, and do not form a company around a solution before confirming a genuine need. Founder-origin problems can strengthen authenticity, but only the founder’s direct experience criterion is explicit in The Pitch material; the broader problem-first rule is operating advice rather than a universal investment gate. [ev:michael-hyatt-0029] [ev:michael-hyatt-0030] [ev:michael-hyatt-0150] [ev:michael-hyatt-0152] [ev:michael-hyatt-0176] [ev:michael-hyatt-0203]

## traction_growth

The decisive validation is customers allocating real budget. An MVP and capable team without customers do not give him enough evidence that a real company exists; referenceable paying B2B customers materially reduce perceived financing risk. [ev:michael-hyatt-0005] [ev:michael-hyatt-0006] [ev:michael-hyatt-0007] [ev:michael-hyatt-0121] [ev:michael-hyatt-0122]

A first purchase matters, but expansion and repeat purchasing are stronger evidence. He repeatedly points to initial revenue followed by customer expansion as the signal that adoption is credible and repeatability is emerging. This is strong validation, not an evidenced universal prerequisite at every stage. [ev:michael-hyatt-0008] [ev:michael-hyatt-0233] [ev:michael-hyatt-0234] [ev:michael-hyatt-0299] [ev:michael-hyatt-0300]

He distinguishes durable growth from purchased growth. Revenue expansion with stable or improving gross margins and controlled cash consumption is attractive; growth accompanied by equivalent losses, discounting, or margin erosion may simply have been bought. [ev:michael-hyatt-0076] [ev:michael-hyatt-0230] [ev:michael-hyatt-0287] [ev:michael-hyatt-0296] [ev:michael-hyatt-0393] [ev:michael-hyatt-0394]

Inferred from his operating counsel, he values a precisely defined, repeatable use case, disciplined enterprise selling, measurable outcomes rather than activity volume, and incremental operating wins such as strong hires, early revenue, distribution, and account expansion. Conference visibility and fundraising celebrations do not substitute for this work. [ev:michael-hyatt-0027] [ev:michael-hyatt-0028] [ev:michael-hyatt-0086] [ev:michael-hyatt-0090] [ev:michael-hyatt-0258] [ev:michael-hyatt-0301] [ev:michael-hyatt-0302]

## business_model_economics

Capital discipline, sound unit economics, and a credible path to profitability are central. Hyatt prefers somewhat slower growth with breakeven economics over maximum growth dependent on continuous financing, and he wants a concrete investment rationale for operating at a loss. [ev:michael-hyatt-0075] [ev:michael-hyatt-0077] [ev:michael-hyatt-0078]

He focuses on early sales, gross margin, and whether the model can ultimately generate net margin. Negative gross margins combined with growth are, in his formulation, mathematically nonviable; free audience-accumulation or land-grab pitches are not sufficient business models. [ev:michael-hyatt-0220] [ev:michael-hyatt-0222] [ev:michael-hyatt-0223] [ev:michael-hyatt-0225] [ev:michael-hyatt-0229]

He has cited gross margins above 70% as important for strong technology companies, but this appears in a particular discussion and should not be promoted into a universal numeric gate. The general rule is to examine user economics, price, gross-margin durability, fixed costs, and the volume required to break even. [ev:michael-hyatt-0149] [ev:michael-hyatt-0289] [ev:michael-hyatt-0395]

Capital should have a productive use. He opposes raising and spending merely because money is available, although he may favor accepting unusually attractive financing while keeping the spending decision separate. Repeated financing can dilute founders into unattractive ownership, so he values resilience, selective fundraising, and preserved founder control. [ev:michael-hyatt-0228] [ev:michael-hyatt-0232] [ev:michael-hyatt-0291] [ev:michael-hyatt-0292] [ev:michael-hyatt-0295] [ev:michael-hyatt-0298]

## deal_terms_valuation

Entry price is fundamental. Hyatt avoids extremely early businesses asking valuations unsupported by operating progress, because even an excellent company can produce a weak investment if bought too expensively. He compares entry value with plausible ordinary exits and considers the extreme exit required by some valuations exceptionally improbable. [ev:michael-hyatt-0067] [ev:michael-hyatt-0073] [ev:michael-hyatt-0226] [ev:michael-hyatt-0227] [ev:michael-hyatt-0293] [ev:michael-hyatt-0294]

He prefers restrained valuations that allow a profitable company to generate attractive realized outcomes without requiring a spectacular headline exit. His private-equity preference is for more repeatable three-to-four-times outcomes rather than a venture portfolio dependent on one unknowable outlier. [ev:michael-hyatt-0101] [ev:michael-hyatt-0102] [ev:michael-hyatt-0103] [ev:michael-hyatt-0231]

Structure and counterparties matter. Unattractive deal structures are explicit reasons not to invest, and he regards large liquidation preferences and severe cap discounts as punitive. He values aligned VC co-investors for additional diligence, shareholder protection, board oversight, and terms alignment. [ev:michael-hyatt-0304] [ev:michael-hyatt-0367] [ev:michael-hyatt-0390] [ev:michael-hyatt-0391]

His direct-company approach favors larger checks concentrated in a few companies, but no reliable universal check-size range is established. Aggregators conflict sharply—one reports $5,000–$50,000 while another reports $1 million–$50 million—and should not override his own description of context-dependent, meaningful checks. [ev:michael-hyatt-0068] [ev:michael-hyatt-0070] [ctx:michael-hyatt-0126] [ctx:michael-hyatt-0116]

## timing

Hyatt has repeatedly viewed adversity as favorable company-formation and selective deployment timing: downturns reduce competition and incumbent attention, while the COVID dislocation accelerated digital adoption and created opportunities for investors with available capital. These are views about environments, not evidence that he mechanically times markets. [ev:michael-hyatt-0001] [ev:michael-hyatt-0015] [ev:michael-hyatt-0139] [ev:michael-hyatt-0370] [ev:michael-hyatt-0371] [ev:michael-hyatt-0372]

Indeed, his family office avoids market timing and underwrites direct investments with an intended holding period of at least ten years. In public equities it follows buy-and-hold behavior rather than active trading, options, or short selling. [ev:michael-hyatt-0276] [ev:michael-hyatt-0278]

His technology forecasts show both conviction and caution. He has anticipated large shifts in AI, autonomous driving, batteries, fintech, and manufactured organs, while acknowledging that identifying a shift does not mean correctly predicting its speed. Quantum appears promising but premature in one discussion. [ev:michael-hyatt-0047] [ev:michael-hyatt-0048] [ev:michael-hyatt-0049] [ev:michael-hyatt-0050] [ev:michael-hyatt-0106] [ev:michael-hyatt-0310]

## investor_fit_constraints

Hyatt is not compelled to deploy a venture fund, so he can reject most opportunities and go long periods without a direct deal. Each startup consumes substantial personal time; the opportunity must justify board work, advice, operational burden, and a decade-long relationship. He may syndicate a round even when able to finance it himself if co-investors add useful capabilities. [ev:michael-hyatt-0019] [ev:michael-hyatt-0020] [ev:michael-hyatt-0021] [ev:michael-hyatt-0022] [ev:michael-hyatt-0268] [ev:michael-hyatt-0269] [ev:michael-hyatt-0378]

His desired contribution is operating experience, not capital alone. He concentrates where his and Richard Hyatt’s skills can materially improve the company, and expects founders to be capable of independently running the business rather than needing elementary instruction. [ev:michael-hyatt-0051] [ev:michael-hyatt-0052] [ev:michael-hyatt-0069] [ev:michael-hyatt-0071] [ev:michael-hyatt-0180] [ev:michael-hyatt-0207]

The family office’s preservation mandate limits risk appetite. Hyatt evaluates returns relative to risk, accepts missing exceptional opportunities, and rejects the idea that entrepreneurial wealth creation proves investment skill. Tax-adjusted return also affects willingness to invest. [ev:michael-hyatt-0181] [ev:michael-hyatt-0183] [ev:michael-hyatt-0184] [ev:michael-hyatt-0187] [ev:michael-hyatt-0188] [ev:michael-hyatt-0105]

A tentative, inferred direct-investment diligence sequence is: research founder and market; test character, coachability, and adaptability; inspect customer budget allocation and repeat expansion; assess differentiation, IP, competition, and category saturation; model gross margin, unit economics, cash requirements, dilution, and a credible ordinary exit; then decide whether terms, ownership, downside, and required personal effort justify the return. This summarizes explicit screens without converting every observation into a mandatory gate. [ev:michael-hyatt-0034] [ev:michael-hyatt-0110] [ev:michael-hyatt-0111] [ev:michael-hyatt-0266] [ev:michael-hyatt-0271] [ev:michael-hyatt-0338] [ev:michael-hyatt-0339]

## Distinctive / doesn't-fit-the-taxonomy

Hyatt’s investment identity is intentionally split. He is simultaneously a patient public-market allocator, an LP in funds, a selective angel or co-investor, a private-equity-oriented control investor, an active company builder, an advisor, and a philanthropist. Rules from one bucket should not automatically migrate to another. The family office has variously been described as dividing capital among public markets, real estate, private equity/venture, and direct acquisitions; other descriptions emphasize public equities, predominantly private equity with limited venture exposure, and operating-company acquisitions. [ctx:michael-hyatt-0002] [ctx:michael-hyatt-0101] [ev:michael-hyatt-0094] [ev:michael-hyatt-0277]

His operating instinct is both advantage and acknowledged bias. He says the traits that made him an entrepreneur initially produced poor investment decisions; good investing requires patience, consistency, rejection, and restraint, including suppressing the urge to intervene continually. Yet his highest-conviction direct investments are precisely those where active help can matter. [ev:michael-hyatt-0091] [ev:michael-hyatt-0092] [ev:michael-hyatt-0093] [ev:michael-hyatt-0153] [ev:michael-hyatt-0259] [ev:michael-hyatt-0330]

His Bitcoin position is another exception: he has owned substantial exposure and used a Fidelity ETF for security, while describing Bitcoin as a trade he dislikes rather than a productive long-term asset. That tactical holding does not imply a long-term crypto thesis. [ctx:michael-hyatt-0034] [ctx:michael-hyatt-0055] [ctx:michael-hyatt-0056] [ctx:michael-hyatt-0058] [ev:michael-hyatt-0398]

Finally, his most celebrated “investment” was helping fund Creative Destruction Lab, which he also characterized as a donation. It reflects ecosystem-building and philanthropic impact rather than a conventional financial holding. [ev:michael-hyatt-0284] [ctx:michael-hyatt-0059] [ctx:michael-hyatt-0274]
