# Sarah Downey — Investment Memory

This memory covers the resolved Sarah A. Downey associated with Accomplice, Yubari, and Blueprint Enneagram. It synthesizes first-person investment writing, attributed interviews, and source-reported context; it excludes same-name people. The record spans changing roles: Accomplice principal with check-writing authority, contract Operating Partner from January 2020 when she said she was no longer writing Accomplice checks, personal angel investor, and founder/GP or co-manager of Yubari. Many source pages lack publication dates, so apparently current language should be treated as current only when written. Advice about operating companies is marked **Inferred** when translated into a possible investment signal. Aggregator claims are not independent verification. Coverage is partial: all 130 available text packets were reviewed, but four downloaded media files lacked transcripts. See [coverage](coverage.md) for the file-level gaps.

## founder_team

Downey’s clearest early-stage hierarchy is “people and market,” with people often decisive because angel investments may precede customers or even a product. She asks whether the team is exceptional, possesses expertise, and has authentic passion for what it is building; she says product strength, exciting markets, and apparently good timing do not compensate for the wrong person. Her two most common personal pass reasons were weak conviction in the founders and insufficient personal conviction in the space. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0098] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0099] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0116] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0119] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0172]

**In:** founders who are trustworthy, gritty, urgent, honest, selfless toward their teams, mission-oriented, curious, action-oriented, and demonstrably exceptional. She looks for a “superpower” in every founder, particularly compelling presence in the public-facing leader, and prizes entrepreneurial work over credentials: independently building or launching something matters more than an MBA, school, conventional degree, appearance, or previous founding experience. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0117] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0240] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0241] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0259] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0308] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0309]

Founder–problem fit should be deep rather than cosmetic. Founders should care intensely about the problem, ideally from firsthand experience, and should not be motivated principally by wealth, power, status, or ego. She tries to distinguish stated from actual motivation through questioning, spontaneous conversation, body language, references, and behavioral evidence. Questions she explicitly uses or endorses include: What are you genuinely passionate about? What would you do in a perfect world? Are you responsible for what you claim to have done? Do you own your faults? [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0155] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0202] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0307] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0321] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0322] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0325]

Team design must fit the business. A technical company should have a technical founder or already-hired senior engineering leader; missing a business-critical skill can independently cause a pass. She ideally likes complementary business and technical founders, while rejecting a universal founder archetype: the relevant test is whether the person’s working style, role, industry, and surrounding team fit one another. A trusted number two with complementary skills can offset a CEO’s weaknesses. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0045] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0154] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0242] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0050] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0051] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0137]

**Out:** substantive dishonesty, fabricated term-sheet urgency, sexist or racist conduct, rudeness, unethical fundraising tactics, and evasive or rambling communication. She describes lying as categorical because the relationship may last decades, and explicitly says sexist pitch behavior means Accomplice would never invest. A rambling first email signals deficient communication, judgment, emotional intelligence, persuasion, and self-regulation. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0042] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0043] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0044] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0132] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0272] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0283]

She expects founders pitching VCs to be working full time, both as evidence of commitment and to avoid intellectual-property risk. Defensiveness, talking over the investor, failure to listen, rigidity, and perfectionism are adverse adaptability signals. Her broader philosophy is to hold strong opinions loosely enough to revise them when worthy new information appears. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0046] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0249] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0210] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0002]

Her people judgment is personal and relational, not presented as a universal scorecard. She invests in people she likes and wants to spend time with and looks for reciprocal values, chemistry, and personality fit. She uses the Enneagram as a non-scientific aid to anticipate team conflict and harmony, not as dispositive evidence. Trusted firsthand experience is strongest; trusted references are next best and lose weight with social distance. She also seeks candid perspectives from employees below the executive layer while explicitly adjusting for bias. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0031] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0120] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0207] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0208] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0143] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0144] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0107] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0109]

Prior failure is not disqualifying. She and her colleagues have backed founders and operators again after unsuccessful companies; what matters is whether they acted with effort, candor, grace, integrity, and respect for employees. She also cautions that market and timing forces can overwhelm good execution. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0218] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0219] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0222] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0223] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0224] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0225]

A major exception runs through her framework: truly exceptional founders, established through personal experience or track record, can overcome nearly every ordinary pass reason. This is an extremely high bar, not permission to ignore concerns casually. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0148] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0262] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0299]

## market_opportunity

Downey seeks venture-scale outliers rather than merely good sustainable businesses. She wants large markets, credible paths to meaningful share, and companies capable of massive outcomes; in one angel framework she described potential for hundreds of millions in revenue in roughly five years, while another example filter used a foreseeable $500 million-plus exit in about five years. Those figures belong to historical/example filters and should not be treated as timeless universal gates. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0149] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0150] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0178] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0188] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0189]

Her question is not simply whether a market exists, but whether the solution is essential, the product can support a standalone company, and the opportunity can return the relevant fund. She independently assesses addressable market rather than accepting founder projections. For the then-$200 million Accomplice fund, she offered a rule of thumb that a prospective exit should at least equal fund size. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0100] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0192] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0193] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0198] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0201]

**In:** category creation, especially businesses forming new industries, or entry into an existing large market with a plausible route to consequential share. **Out or wary:** products that are features rather than companies, crowded categories requiring expensive battles for visibility and users, and markets too small for venture economics. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0187] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0189] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0163] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0153]

## competition_defensibility

Claims of “no competition” are close to disqualifying: they imply inadequate market research or unjustified confidence. Even category creators should identify analogous companies and relevant paths. Her diligence question is therefore: Who are the direct competitors or best analogues, and why is this company difficult to copy? [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0037] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0245]

She values differentiated, technically difficult products and worries when an incumbent or new entrant can reproduce the entire concept readily. Technical depth can contribute to defensibility, but patents are neither essential nor sufficient; patent portfolios can consume time and money without building the business. Vesper illustrates what she found compelling: rugged microphones, novel low-power wake-word functionality, protected design methods, and a claimed long technical lead. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0194] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0266] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0267] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0268] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0165] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0253] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0254] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0257]

A direct conflict with an existing portfolio company can block investment. Scar tissue also matters: past failures, including indirectly observed ones, can bias investors against similar businesses, a bias she describes rather than endorses as a universal rule. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0280] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0278]

## product_solution

The product must do what the company claims and solve an important “painkiller” problem. For highly technical products, she recommends independent expert validation when the investor lacks the necessary expertise. **Inferred:** founders should put products in real users’ hands, observe behavior as well as stated feedback, and develop users into ongoing development partners; this is strong validation, not an expressly mandatory prerequisite at every stage. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0164] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0170] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0199] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0060] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0061] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0062] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0063]

In frontier products, she rewards genuinely new interaction models rather than ports of familiar interfaces. She has emphasized accessibility, openness, universality, ease of use, low resource consumption without degraded experience, and fitting existing user behavior. Product delight and network presence can sometimes overcome privacy-preserving onboarding friction. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0092] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0067] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0068] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0028] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0022] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0023]

Privacy is a substantive product and business-model lens. She favors privacy by design, data minimization, and products that avoid monetizing users and their data; retrofitting privacy later is materially harder. She explicitly invited founders building a genuinely private metaverse or virtual environment to contact her. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0018] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0019] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0020] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0021] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0214]

## traction_growth

Traction is stage- and company-specific: users, downloads, paying customers, revenue, partnerships, and product milestones may all count. At pre-seed she expected early customer conversations but not significant revenue; at seed, product, user, or revenue milestones can demonstrate progress; her Series A description emphasized substantial traction across product quality, engagement, acquisition, network effects, customer value, pipeline, or revenue growth. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0166] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0311] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0312] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0313]

Revenue is not always necessary. She explicitly said she could accept pre-revenue status when users are present, because monetization can be addressed later. This should be read as willingness, not a universal preference for growth without economics. She still expects a plausible go-to-market plan and rejects “launch and users will find us” reasoning. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0176] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0167]

She accepts nonlinear progress: strong companies experience rifts, pivots, and near-death periods. Those events are not automatically adverse, but reliance on one customer, one knowledge holder, one supplier, or one financing source creates material survival risk. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0007] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0177] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0054] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0055] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0056]

## business_model_economics

The venture model should multiply revenue without proportionate labor or incremental cost. She is skeptical of studios and high-touch services whose growth requires continually adding people, and of hit-driven content businesses such as VR content or pure gaming titles. She distinguishes those from gaming infrastructure and enabling software, which fit her stated interests better. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0169] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0026] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0027] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0029] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0286]

Founders should command the assumptions behind customer value, acquisition cost, pricing, burn, and financing needs rather than pretend long-range forecasts are precise. Weak economics damage viability; false metrics additionally destroy credibility. She expects a reasoned use-of-funds plan tied to milestones and time. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0243] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0246] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0171]

Her view is not that every good company should take venture money. She explicitly warns that most good businesses are not venture-scale and can be harmed by investor obligations when bootstrapping would suffice; her own Blueprint/Atrous plan was a lifestyle business without fundraising unless extraordinary traction justified reconsideration. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0235] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0236] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0237]

## deal_terms_valuation

Company selection dominates terms in power-law outcomes: she says people and market matter most, while paperwork and terms matter principally in losing or roughly flat results. Nonetheless, she considers both extremely high and extremely low valuations dangerous. High prices reduce ownership and raise the next-round hurdle; low prices can signal poor judgment and may leave founders insufficiently motivated after excessive dilution. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0102] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0038] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0039] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0250] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0281]

Convertible debt is simpler, faster, and cheaper because it defers formal valuation, although she notes that valuation caps can have nearly the same economic effect. She regards uncapped notes as generally bad for angels, values pro rata rights, and notes that successful follow-ons may quickly become unaffordable for a small angel. A historical example filter used a $10 million-or-less pre-money valuation or cap, rejected uncapped notes, and sought preferred shares; it was an example filter, not necessarily her permanent personal mandate. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0079] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0082] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0085] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0086] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0087] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0179] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0180] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0181]

She warns against concentrated control: a lone investor owning roughly 40% can make future financings depend heavily on that investor’s continued support. Two co-leads can diversify that dependence. Strategic corporate investors may introduce incentives beyond financial return. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0057] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0058] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0059] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0160]

## timing

“Is now the right time?” is explicit diligence. Too early means years of R&D, unavailable demand, and repeated financing before the market pays; too late means saturation and little room for a new entrant. She favors industries approaching increased investment and acquisition interest, but distinguishes favorable financing conditions from actual customer readiness. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0101] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0190] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0191] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0265]

Her frontier writing repeatedly separates long-term conviction from present investability. She anticipated AR/VR convergence and broad future AR applications, yet judged functional haptics too early because adoption, technology, and developer ecosystems were immature. Construction appeared a more reachable AR beachhead than consumer adoption because it had immediate use cases and purchasing capacity. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0122] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0124] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0329] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0112] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0115]

## investor_fit_constraints

Her explicit thematic fit centers on frontier technology—science-fiction-like, difficult technical leaps—especially AR/VR, next-generation gaming, AI, robotics, identity/privacy, enabling hardware, and future consumer health. She prefers massive risky advances over minor improvements and personally needs interest or conviction in the technology. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0073] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0074] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0075] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0076] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0127] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0172]

Within VR she explicitly preferred middleware, streaming infrastructure, 3D modeling, content-creation tools, and connective software. She ruled out headsets because of capital intensity and crowding, and viewed content as difficult because it is exceptionally hit-driven. In another historical taxonomy she excluded or questioned life sciences, medical devices, biotech, simple e-commerce, capital-intensive businesses, pure gaming companies, editorial media, consumer packaged goods, and heavily offline businesses. These statements are category- and period-specific rather than proof of permanent exclusions. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0024] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0025] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0026] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0027] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0029] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0286]

Mandate changed with role. As an Accomplice principal, she reported authority to make seed investments up to $2.5 million with one investment-team colleague’s concurrence, and described first checks of $1 million–$2.5 million for 10%–20% ownership. From January 2020 she said she became a contract Operating Partner, continued boards and portfolio work, but stopped writing Accomplice checks. Yubari later wrote $10,000–$100,000 pre-seed checks, mostly in frontier areas such as gaming, crypto, and digital health. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0134] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0135] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0159] [ctx:sarah-downey-accomplice-0124] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0232]

Capital intensity must fit the capital provider. Hardware can cost more and take longer than expected; larger funds may be better suited to such companies. This is a financing-risk warning, not a universal refusal—Vesper was a hardware investment she co-sponsored, and her sector list includes enabling hardware. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0158] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0168] [ctx:sarah-downey-accomplice-0144] [ctx:sarah-downey-accomplice-0145] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0075]

Sourcing favors trusted access but is not closed. Portfolio or network introductions carry more weight than pitch events, yet she values a thoughtful, personalized cold email. Cold outreach has historically converted rarely at Accomplice, so the email should be brief, explain specific investor fit, establish founder identity and the high-level idea, and create curiosity without deception. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0005] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0006] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0284] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0315] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0316] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0319]

Her preferred post-investment role is empathetic, trusted support: capital, advice, introductions, hiring and fundraising help, especially during crises, while staying out of daily minutiae. She questions whether very early companies need formal boards and judges board contributions by whether they help with an important issue rather than display the investor’s importance. ## Distinctive / doesn't-fit-the-taxonomy

Downey combines unusually explicit personal chemistry with structured venture logic. She openly invests in people she likes and technology she personally wants to exist, while also insisting on market size, timing, defensibility, scalability, and fund fit. Founder enthusiasm can sometimes create interest in a category that looked unappealing on paper, and exceptional people or ideas can override most ordinary objections. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0031] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0261] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0275] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0299]

Her science-fiction habit is not merely branding: she uses speculative fiction to imagine technologies outside present feasibility, but her timing discipline prevents future inevitability from becoming automatic present investability. This produces a characteristic tension—strong enthusiasm for a future such as haptics, AR, VR, translation, or private virtual worlds, paired with willingness to pass when the ecosystem, distribution, or capital path is premature. [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0212] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0226] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0229] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0329] [ev:sarah-downey-accomplice-0330]

Her own operating choices also impose a useful boundary on the memory: venture backing is not synonymous with business quality. Autonomy, bootstrapping, and lifestyle-company design can be correct when the opportunity is not venture-scale. Accordingly, this memory should not convert her venture filters into judgments that a rejected company is a bad company.
